California and the Bundled-Payment Experiment

California is not just another state on the roster of Medicare's newest payment experiment. It is the roster's center of gravity. When the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services drew up the hospitals required to join the Transforming Episode Accountability Model, or TEAM, it put more of them in California than in any other state: one hundred seven hospitals, spread across eleven metropolitan markets, committed to five years of a program that just began. Only one hospital on that list is there by choice. The other hundred and six were mandated in.

To understand why that matters, it helps to understand what TEAM actually asks of a hospital. Historically, Medicare paid for surgery the way it paid for everything else: a fee for the operation, another fee for the hospital stay, and separate fees for whatever came after, whether that was a skilled nursing facility, home health care, or a readmission. TEAM changes the unit of account. A hospital is now judged, and paid, on the full thirty-day arc that follows five kinds of procedures: hip and knee replacement, spinal fusion, bypass surgery, hip fracture repair, and major bowel surgery. Spend too much, or let a patient bounce back into the hospital, and the model treats that as the hospital's problem to solve, not a downstream cost for someone else to absorb.

So where does California actually stand two years into a program built around this five-year clock? The geography is the first surprise. Nearly two-thirds of the state's hospitals sit inside just two metro areas. The San Francisco Bay Area, at thirty-seven hospitals, is the second-largest TEAM market in the entire country, trailing only New York. The Inland Empire, anchored around Riverside and San Bernardino, comes in fourth nationally with thirty. Add San Diego, and three markets account for most of the state's exposure. Greater Los Angeles, by contrast, barely registers: Cedars-Sinai is the only hospital CMS selected there, a reminder that the model was built around specific metro statistical areas, not around hospital density or population.

The more interesting story is what the data says about how these hospitals are actually performing, because it cuts in two directions at once. Medicare tracks something called an excess readmission ratio for every hospital: essentially, how a hospital's actual readmission rate compares to what you'd expect given the patients it treats. A score below one means fewer readmissions than predicted. Above one means more.

On the two surgical categories that map most directly onto what TEAM pays for, hip and knee replacement and bypass surgery, California's hospitals are outperforming the rest of the country. Their readmission ratio for joint replacement sits meaningfully below the national average; the same is true for bypass. That is genuinely good news heading into a model where surgical episodes are the whole point.

But flip to the medical side of the ledger and the picture reverses. On pneumonia, heart failure, and heart attack, three conditions that aren't part of TEAM's five surgical categories but draw on the exact same hospital infrastructure, California runs worse than the national average, not better. Pneumonia stands out in particular, with a noticeably higher share of hospitals landing above the expected readmission line than you'd see nationally.

That split is not just a curiosity. It is a preview of where the model is heading. In its first year, TEAM's quality scoring leaned partly on a patient-safety measure that is being retired. Starting in the program's second year, more weight shifts onto a broader readmission measure, one that does not distinguish between a surgical patient and a medical one. The exact infrastructure that is currently pulling California's numbers down on pneumonia and heart failure is about to count for more, not less, inside the score that determines how the model treats a hospital financially.

There is also a note of caution buried in the averages themselves. California's strong showing on joint replacement is not a story of every hospital doing well. Roughly half of the state's reporting hospitals are still sitting above the expected line on that very measure. The statewide average looks good because a handful of high performers are pulling it down, not because performance is uniform. For any individual hospital trying to figure out where it actually stands, the state number is close to useless. The only number that matters is its own.

A few other changes are already locked in and worth knowing about. Spinal fusion, one of TEAM's five procedure categories from the start, is getting more granular: new diagnosis codes for the most extensive and complex fusion surgeries take effect this coming October, and hospitals that haven't updated their coding and documentation workflows for that will feel it at reconciliation. The way Medicare calculates target prices is also getting recalibrated, pulling in a full three years of baseline data instead of just the most recent year, which should smooth out some of the year-to-year noise hospitals have been navigating.

And there's a wrinkle worth watching even for hospitals that think TEAM is their only concern. Washington finalized a second, separate mandatory bundled-payment program for joint replacement, this one covering nearly every acute-care hospital in the country starting in 2028, explicitly carving out the hospitals already inside TEAM. The practical effect is that California's TEAM hospitals will keep operating on a thirty-day episode window through the end of the decade, while most of their peers elsewhere move to a ninety-day window that also covers outpatient procedures. Two very different playbooks, running side by side, and worth remembering the next time a joint-replacement strategy gets compared across state lines or across a merger.

None of this comes with a price tag, and that omission is deliberate rather than an oversight. Medicare doesn't publish what a given hospital's target price is, or what its baseline episode spending looks like, and without that, any dollar figure floating around about California's exposure is someone's guess dressed up as a number. The honest version of this story is the one told in ratios and rankings, not invented dollar signs.

Put simply: California enters the second year of the country's newest bundled-payment model as its single largest test case, doing better than the rest of the nation on exactly the procedures the model was built around, and worse on the everyday medical admissions that share the same hallways, the same discharge planners, and the same post-acute partners. The score is about to start counting both. The hospitals that treat this as one continuous discharge problem, rather than two separate ones, are the ones most likely to come out ahead when the next reconciliation lands.
