# Medicare Picked TEAM's Markets by Lottery. Nobody Else Does This.

*England, Germany, and China all mandated bundled or DRG-based hospital payment nationwide, all at once. Medicare tested it on a randomly drawn subset of the country instead — twice. The reason is a specific line in US law, not a preference for fairness.*

Rainfall Health · September 2026

**In brief.** TEAM's CBSA lottery isn't a one-off design quirk — it's the second time CMS has used stratified random sampling to pick which markets face a mandatory bundled-payment model. It did the same thing for CJR in 2016. Los Angeles was drawn into that one. It wasn't drawn into TEAM. No other major national health system does this. England, Germany, and China all rolled out their versions of bundled or case-based payment as a uniform national rule, applied everywhere, all at once. The gap comes down to one thing: Medicare's Innovation Center is legally required to *test* a payment model before it can expand it nationwide. Section 1115A of the Social Security Act is the mandate. A test needs a control group. A control group needs a lottery.

## CMS has done this exact thing before

The lottery mechanism behind TEAM — random draw, stratified by market characteristics, some places selected and some left out — is not unique to TEAM. It's the same method CMS used for the Comprehensive Care for Joint Replacement (CJR) model in 2016: 67 metropolitan statistical areas, stratified into eight groups by historical episode payment and population size, and randomly selected within each stratum. CMS designed it this way specifically to create a control group — 104 MSAs that were eligible but not selected, used to measure what would have happened without the program.

Los Angeles was drawn into that lottery. It's one of the 67 original CJR markets. Then, in 2018, when CMS narrowed mandatory participation from 67 MSAs down to the 34 with the highest historical payments — not a second random draw, a selection on cost — LA stayed in, because it was one of the 34. It spent all nine years of CJR as a mandatory market.

So when TEAM ran its own version of the same lottery in 2024, LA had already been through this process once and won. The second time, it lost. Same city, same mechanism, opposite outcome, eight years apart.

## England doesn't do this

The NHS's equivalent to bundled payment for joint replacement is the Best Practice Tariff (BPT) — a pricing adjustment that pays more for hip and knee replacement when a hospital hits outcome thresholds tracked through the National Joint Registry and patient-reported outcome measures. It applies to every NHS trust in England. There's no selected subset, no control group, no market left out to compare against. It's been running since 2014 and gets updated annually in the NHS Payment Scheme, most recently for 2026/27. Every hospital in the system either meets the criteria or it doesn't; none sits outside the policy because of a draw.

## Germany didn't do this either

Germany's G-DRG system — case-based hospital payment, the rough equivalent of what CMS's own inpatient DRG system does domestically — went from optional to mandatory in exactly one year. Hospitals could voluntarily group patients into DRGs starting in 2003. In 2004, it became mandatory for every hospital in the country, with the narrow exception of psychiatric and psychosomatic units. No geographic pilot, no randomized subset — one year of opt-in, then a nationwide switch for everyone at once.

## Neither did China

China's National Healthcare Security Administration mandated DRG-based bundled payment nationwide starting January 2021. Same pattern: a national administrative mandate applied everywhere, not a selected sample of provinces or cities.

## Why the US does it differently

This isn't a matter of American regulators being more rigorous or more cautious than their counterparts elsewhere. It's a specific legal constraint. CMS's Innovation Center exists under Section 1115A of the Social Security Act, and that section's purpose is to let CMS *test* payment and service-delivery models before deciding whether to expand them nationally. Testing, in the way CMS's own economists and outside evaluators define it, means having a comparison group — markets that didn't get the policy, so the effect of the policy can be measured against what would have happened anyway. England's Department of Health and Social Care doesn't operate under an equivalent legal requirement to run a controlled experiment before setting national tariff policy. Neither does Germany's self-governing hospital-and-insurer body, nor China's healthcare security administration. They mandate the policy directly. CMS, by statute, has to test it first — and a real test needs a group left out.

That's the actual mechanism behind Los Angeles losing the TEAM lottery. It's not politics, and it's not a judgment about LA specifically. But it's also not an administrative accident unique to TEAM. It's what US law requires CMS's Innovation Center to do every time it wants to mandate a new payment model — and CJR proves this exact office already ran this exact play once, with this exact city, with the opposite result.

## What's still unverified

Whether CJR's control-group MSAs (the 104 eligible but not drawn) were ever directly compared to TEAM's excluded CBSAs — whether this is the same underlying sampling frame reused, or two independently built samples that happen to use the same technique — is not confirmed here. A natural follow-up: pull CJR's original 171-MSA eligible pool and TEAM's 803 eligible CBSAs, and measure how much overlaps as "eligible both times, drawn neither time."
