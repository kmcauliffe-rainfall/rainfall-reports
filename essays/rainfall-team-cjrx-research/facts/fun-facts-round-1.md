Fun facts, round 1 — international systems, TEAM/CJR-X mechanics, odd details

1. Germany's DRG system isn't German. When Germany built the G-DRG system in 2000, its hospital and insurer associations picked the Australian Refined DRG system (AR-DRG v4.1) as the base and adapted it, rather than building a classification system from scratch.

2. The whole concept of DRGs started at Yale, not in government. Diagnosis-Related Groups were developed at Yale University in the 1970s (Robert Fetter and John Thompson) as a way to measure hospital case-mix, years before Medicare adopted DRGs for inpatient payment in 1983.

3. Germany's national switch took exactly one year. Optional in 2003, mandatory for every hospital in the country in 2004, with only psychiatric and psychosomatic units exempted. No phased regions, no pilot markets.

4. CJR's 2016 lottery deliberately kept 104 markets untouched, on purpose. Those weren't leftovers — CMS reserved them as a control group to measure what would have happened in CJR-type markets without the program.

5. The 2018 CJR downsizing wasn't a second lottery. CMS cut mandatory participation from 67 markets to 34 by selecting the highest historical spenders, a selection rule, not a random draw. Only the first cut was chance; the second was math.

6. CJR-X got a three-month reprieve nobody asked for. Proposed to start October 1, 2027, the final rule pushed it to January 1, 2028 instead — later than proposed, not earlier.

7. Maryland is exempt from CJR-X entirely, not because of anything TEAM-related, but because it runs its own unique all-payer hospital rate-setting system, separate from the rest of the country's Medicare payment rules.

8. TEAM's own presenters couldn't agree on the name of a program they were presenting. On the same CMS webcast, the "Decarbonization and Resilience Initiative" gets called the "Decarbonization and Climate Resilience Initiative" eleven minutes later, by the same person.

9. A hospital's carbon footprint reporting under TEAM has zero financial effect. Disclosure only, doesn't touch target price, quality score, or reconciliation.

10. TEAM has a built-in hurricane clause. If a hospital sits in a federally declared disaster area during a defined emergency window, the episode gets cancelled and the hospital owes nothing for it. CMS used Hurricane Helene as its own hypothetical example on the call.

11. TEAM's target prices bake in social deprivation, not just medical complexity. Alongside age and comorbidities, CMS adjusts pricing using the Area Deprivation Index, a neighborhood-level poverty/hardship measure, folded directly into a surgical payment formula.

12. There's a federal anti-kickback exemption written specifically for this. Gainsharing between a hospital and the surgeons feeding it patients would normally risk violating the Anti-Kickback Statute; CMS built TEAM with an explicit safe harbor so those payments are legal.

13. The original CJR model covered ankle replacement too. Almost every description says "hip and knee." The outpatient version also included total ankle replacement, a detail that gets dropped constantly in secondary coverage.

14. TEAM has a built-in anti-gaming clause for what happens after the 30 days end. If a hospital's spending in the 30 days immediately following an episode is more than three standard deviations above the regional average, CMS claws that excess back, closing the loophole of pushing costs a few weeks downstream.

15. CMS's own national hospital count for TEAM doesn't match its own tracker. The main CMS TEAM page says 720 hospitals; a CMS-adjacent tracking page says 721, sourced to a different snapshot date of the same "Q2 2026" file.

16. Kaiser Permanente isn't even the biggest single-name system in TEAM nationally. Kaiser holds 19 hospitals nationwide, more than AdventHealth (16) or Baptist (14), but the "South" census region as a whole carries 242 hospitals, by far the largest of the four regions.

17. England ties surgical payment to a patient registry, not a claims database. The NHS's Best Practice Tariff for hip and knee replacement is verified against the National Joint Registry, a clinical outcomes database that exists independently of the billing system.

18. TEAM's SNF waiver has a quality floor built in. Patients can skip the usual 3-day inpatient stay requirement before Medicare will cover a nursing-facility stay, but only if that nursing facility held a 3-star (or better) CMS rating for 7 of the last 12 months.

19. China's national DRG mandate and TEAM share almost the exact same launch window. China's nationwide DRG mandate took effect January 2021; TEAM's own baseline period, the three years of spending data used to set its prices, starts January 2022.

20. The original CJR final rule drew formal legal objections over whether CMS even had the authority to force participation at all. Commenters argued Congress meant model tests to stay voluntary, and that mandating something at this scale blurred the line between "testing" and quietly expanding a program without a vote. That argument didn't win, and it's the same legal question CJR-X and TEAM inherit today.
