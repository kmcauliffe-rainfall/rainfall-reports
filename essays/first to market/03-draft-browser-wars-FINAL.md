# Why the Company That Killed Netscape Died the Same Way

**Status: most recent draft, verified against primary sources for its
major claims. Treat as the current working draft, not a final. See
02-sources-verified.md for citations behind every specific claim below.**

---

The web has had three browsers hold a dominant, unchallenged lead:
Netscape, Internet Explorer, Chrome. Each one took the crown from the
browser before it using the identical weakness. Nobody fixed the flaw
between rounds. They just inherited it.

The reason isn't a better browser winning each time, and it isn't luck
running out twice for the same company. It's a mistake buried in how
"winning" gets measured, one that two Stanford researchers flagged in
writing back in 1988, and that the industry kept making anyway.

The board, first.

Netscape Navigator held roughly 90 percent of the browser market in 1996.
It was the pioneer in the fullest sense: first widely used browser, first
to turn the web into something ordinary people opened daily, first to
IPO on the strength of it, hitting a 3 billion dollar valuation sixteen
months after founding. Microsoft entered after Netscape had already
defined the category, bundling Internet Explorer into Windows starting
in 1995. The clincher wasn't the software. It was a March 1996 deal
giving AOL, then the internet's front door for millions of households, a
folder on the Windows desktop in exchange for making IE the default
browser instead of Navigator. A 1998 internal Microsoft document, later
entered as a government exhibit in the antitrust trial, put it plainly:
"It came with my computer, is the number one reason people switch to
IE." By early 1999, Netscape's share had fallen to 42 percent against
IE's climb past 55. By 2001, Netscape was in single digits and IE held
roughly 90.

IE inherited the crown. It did not inherit a moat.

Internet Explorer won by controlling distribution, not by building
anything that made users unable to leave. A browser costs nothing to
abandon. Once Microsoft could no longer force IE onto every desktop,
once AOL stopped being the internet's front door and antitrust scrutiny
constrained the bundling itself, the exact vulnerability that killed
Netscape sat there, unaddressed, in Microsoft's own product. Firefox took
a real bite starting in 2004. In 2008, Chrome arrived, entering third
into a category two other companies had already defined, at a moment
when IE's share had already slipped to around 74 percent. Chrome needed
the same thing IE originally had over Netscape: a faster product, and a
userbase with nothing tying them to the incumbent. By around 2012, Chrome
had overtaken Internet Explorer. Today it holds over 70 percent of the
global market, a number close to what Netscape held the year Microsoft
entered, and what IE held the year Google did.

The mistake underneath all three rounds.

In 1988, Marvin Lieberman and David Montgomery published the paper that
first mapped out how first-mover advantage actually works, and buried in
it is a warning that the field spent the next few decades half-ignoring.
They defined the advantage strictly: positive economic profit, meaning
profit above the cost of capital, not market share, not survival, not
brand recognition. Then they showed why that distinction matters with a
simple example. Take two identical firms growing at the same rate,
earning zero excess profit, no real advantage to either one. Firm A
enters the market one year before Firm B. Firm A will show a market
share lead over Firm B forever, purely from the year's head start, even
though by the paper's own definition there is no first-mover advantage
present at all. Market share, in other words, can look exactly like a
durable edge and be nothing but arithmetic.

That's why so much of the pioneer-versus-follower argument talks past
itself. Researchers measuring market share kept finding a pioneer
advantage. Researchers measuring survival or actual profit found it far
less reliably, or found the opposite. Peter Golder and Gerard Tellis ran
into this directly in 1993, tracing roughly 500 brands across fifty
categories using historical trade records instead of survey data.
Pioneers failed at 47 percent overall, worse for durable goods at 67
percent, better for nondurable goods at 28 percent. Surviving pioneers
averaged 10 percent market share. Firms that entered early but not
first, what they called early leaders, averaged 28 percent share, an 8
percent failure rate, and entered the category thirteen years after the
pioneer on average. Only four of the thirty-six categories they studied
still had the original pioneer in the lead decades later, and even those
four had an asterisk. Coca-Cola's early dominance likely owed something
to the cocaine and caffeine in the original 1886 formula driving repeat
purchase, not brand loyalty in the way the theory intends.

Golder and Tellis also caught something that mirrors Lieberman and
Montgomery's own warning about definitions. A prior study had named
Miller Lite as light beer's first entrant. It wasn't. Trommer's Red
Letter beat it to market by fourteen years. Even the professionals get
who was first wrong when they don't check.

Real names, when pioneering goes bad.

Lieberman and Montgomery didn't just theorize the downside, they named
it. Bowmar and Osborne, pioneers in electronic calculators and portable
computers, are cited by name as cases where the net effect of being
first was negative. Docutel invented the automated teller machine and
supplied essentially the entire market through 1974. Within four years
its share had fallen under 10 percent, run over by Honeywell, IBM, and
Burroughs, who arrived with full electronic banking systems once the
category proved itself. EMI built the first CT scanner and lost the
market anyway, because it had no medical sales infrastructure and no
manufacturing base in the field, the exact assets that let a later mover
convert a good idea into a durable business.

The pattern connecting Bowmar, Osborne, Docutel, EMI, Netscape, and
Internet Explorer is the same one Lieberman and Montgomery set out to
map. Whoever locks down a real structural asset, control of a scarce
input, a patent that holds up, or a cost real enough that customers
won't pay it to leave, keeps what they built. Whoever doesn't just holds
a number that looks like a lead until someone else shows up and takes
it, the same way, for the same reason, with nothing standing in the way
the second time either.

The same test, running right now.

OpenAI built the AI category. As of this September, it's delaying its
own IPO past 2026 over safety concerns, while Anthropic, founded four
years after OpenAI shipped its first product, has overtaken it in
annualized revenue and is proceeding toward a listing this fall. Nobody
has run the thirty-year study on this one yet. The pattern already has
three other names on it.
