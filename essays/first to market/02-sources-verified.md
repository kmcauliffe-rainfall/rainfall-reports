## Golder, Peter N. and Gerard J. Tellis (1993)
**"Pioneer Advantage: Marketing Logic or Marketing Legend?"**
*Journal of Marketing Research*, Vol. 30, No. 2 (May 1993), pp. 158-170.
Full text: https://gtellis.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/do-pioneers-really-have-long-term-advantage-a-historical-analysis..pdf
**Status: read in full.**

Method: historical analysis of ~500 brands / 50 product categories, using
periodicals and books written contemporaneously with each market's
emergence (avoids survivor bias and self-report bias of prior PIMS/ASSESSOR
studies).

Four definitions used in the paper:
- **Inventor**: firm(s) that develop patents or key technologies in a new
  category.
- **Product pioneer**: first firm to build a working model/sample.
- **Market pioneer**: first firm to actually sell in the category.
- **Early leader**: the firm with the largest share during the category's
  early growth phase (not necessarily the pioneer).

Worked example from the paper itself (mainframe computers): ENIAC
(University of Pennsylvania) = product pioneer. Remington-Rand's UNIVAC,
sold to the Census Bureau in 1951, = market pioneer. IBM entered in 1953
with a sale to Los Alamos and had established dominance by 1955.

Key numbers (36 categories with full data, from Tables 4-7):
- Overall pioneer failure rate: **47%** (17 of 36 categories)
  - Pre-WWII: 50% / Post-WWII: 44%
  - **Durable goods: 67% / Nondurable goods: 28%**
- Mean market share of pioneers: **10%** overall
  - Pre-WWII: 13% / Post-WWII: 7%
  - Durable goods: 7% / Nondurable goods: 13%
  - (Surviving pioneers only, excluding failures: 19%)
- Pioneers who are still current market leaders: **11%** (4 of 36
  categories) — and even those 4 have caveats (see below)
- Early leaders (entered ~13 years after the pioneer on average — 19
  years pre-WWII, 5 years post-WWII):
  - Failure rate: **8%**
  - Mean market share: **28%**
  - Still market leaders today: **53%** of categories

Caveats on the "4 surviving pioneer-leader" categories:
- Coca-Cola: original 1886 formula contained cocaine (coca leaf extract)
  and caffeine; the paper suggests this may have driven repeat purchase
  behavior "as much as any other factor posited by theories supporting a
  pioneer advantage" — i.e., possibly not brand loyalty in the theorized
  sense.
- Telephone/AT&T: Bell only secured market dominance after settling a
  patent-infringement dispute with Western Union, paying Western Union 20%
  of revenues for 17 years.
- Color TV/RCA: GE eventually sold its color TV business to Thomson
  Electronics because it wasn't profitable enough, despite RCA/the
  category having "won."

Specific correction the paper makes to prior research: a previous study
(Urban et al. 1986) identified **Miller Lite (1975)** as light beer's first
entrant. Golder & Tellis found the actual pioneer was **Trommer's Red
Letter (1961)** — 14 years earlier. Also flagged: popular belief credits
Goodyear as tire/rubber pioneer (because Charles Goodyear discovered
vulcanization), but the Goodyear company was founded decades after his
death and decades after Goodrich actually entered the market. Similarly,
Apple is popularly treated as the personal computer pioneer, but was
preceded by MITS and dozens of other companies.

Good anecdote from the paper: quotes from *Financial World* in the 1960s
("World's biggest chain of highway restaurants," "Pioneer in restaurant
franchising," "Most fabulous success story in restaurant chains") sound
like they describe McDonald's. They describe Howard Johnson's. Used by the
authors to illustrate how legend outpaces fact over time.

Important limitation the paper itself flags: controlling for other
variables (positioning, advertising, relative product quality, price)
shrinks the "order of entry" effect in related studies, and in one case
(Robinson & Fornell's model) flips it slightly negative (not statistically
significant). The authors are explicit that pioneering's effect on
performance is entangled with execution quality and can't be fully
separated from it.

## Lieberman, Marvin B. and David B. Montgomery (1988)
**"First-Mover Advantages"**
*Strategic Management Journal*, Vol. 9 (Summer 1988), pp. 41-58.
DOI: 10.1002/smj.4250090706
**Status: read in full** (via a course-upload mirror of the paper text;
citation/DOI confirmed independently via Wiley Online Library).

Core definitional point: first-mover advantage is defined strictly as
**positive economic profit** (profit in excess of the cost of capital) —
explicitly NOT market share, NOT survival, NOT brand recognition. The
paper argues these are commonly-used but potentially misleading proxies.

**The thought experiment** (useful, concrete, worth quoting/adapting
directly): two identical firms, A and B, growing at the same rate (e.g.
20%/year), both earning zero excess economic profit — i.e., no real
first-mover advantage exists between them by the paper's own definition.
Firm A enters the market exactly one year before Firm B. Firm A will show
a permanent ~20% market-share lead over Firm B, forever, purely as an
artifact of the one-year head start — despite there being no real
advantage underneath it. This is the paper's own illustration of why
market-share-based studies of "pioneer advantage" can be measuring an
artifact rather than a real effect.

Three named mechanisms that can make a first-mover advantage durable:
1. **Technological leadership** — learning/experience curve effects kept
   proprietary, or success in patent/R&D races.
2. **Preemption of scarce assets** — locking up scarce physical resources,
   prime locations, or input factors before rivals notice their value.
3. **Buyer switching costs** — costs (financial, workflow, retraining)
   that make it expensive for a customer to leave once they've adopted the
   pioneer's product.

Named case examples used in the paper (real companies, cited by name):
- **Xerox**: used a patent thicket around xerography (not just the core
  patent, but a wide defensive set of related patents) as an entry
  barrier until challengers used antitrust action to force compulsory
  licensing.
- **GE**: long-term dominance of the electric lamp industry initially from
  controlling the basic Edison patent, later sustained via hundreds of
  minor patents on the lamp and associated equipment.
- **Wal-Mart**: geographic preemption — targeted small southern towns
  competitors found unprofitable to service, paired with an efficient
  distribution network, to defend its position and sustain high profits.
- **The "Big Three" cereal companies**: subject of an FTC lawsuit alleging
  they preempted supermarket shelf space and product-differentiation
  niches to sustain high profits. The suit was dismissed, but the firms
  continued to sustain exceptionally high profit rates.
- **EMI**: developed the first CT scanner but lost the market because it
  lacked a technology infrastructure and marketing base in the medical
  field.
- **Pilkington**: by contrast, profited handsomely from its pioneering
  float-glass process because it already had relevant assets and
  experience in the glass industry.
- **IBM (personal computers)** and **Matsushita (VCRs)**: cited as
  late-movers who succeeded largely because they could exploit existing
  assets in marketing, distribution, and customer reputation.
- **Docutel**: invented the automated teller machine and supplied
  virtually the entire ATM market through late 1974. Within the next four
  years, its market share fell to under 10% under competition from
  Honeywell, IBM, and Burroughs, who offered full computer systems meeting
  the emerging need for electronic funds transfer.
- **Bowmar** (electronic calculators) and **Osborne** (portable computers):
  named explicitly, by the paper's authors, as pioneers whose "net impact"
  of being first "may well [have been] negative."
- **Kleenex**: lost significant market share in the late 1970s when
  production-cost choices (increased recycled wood pulp) made the product
  less soft than competitors, shifting consumer preference away; regained
  and surpassed prior share once softness was restored.
- **Tohatsu**: leading Japanese motorcycle producer in the late 1950s;
  failed to expand capacity sufficiently in a high-growth market and was
  overtaken by Honda.
- **Toyota**: interviewed owners of Volkswagens (the leading small car in
  the U.S. at the time) before designing its own car for the U.S. market —
  cited as an example of a late-mover benefiting from the pioneer's
  established mistakes/lessons.
- **Matsushita**: nicknamed in Japan *maneshita denki*, "electronics that
  have been copied" — the company generally let Sony innovate first, then
  entered based on manufacturing and marketing capability. Sony, by
  contrast, "aggressively pursues first-mover advantages from new product
  innovation."

Counter-evidence also cited in the same paper (important for balance —
this is not a one-sided "pioneers always lose" paper):
- Ries and Trout (1986): of 25 leading brands in 1923, 20 were still in
  first place roughly 60 years later.
- Robinson (1988) and Robinson & Fornell (1985): order of entry explained
  18% of the variance in market share in consumer goods markets, but only
  8% in industrial goods markets — i.e., pioneer advantage effects are
  real in some studies, just much stronger in some contexts than others,
  and the effect size itself is contested and context-dependent.

## VanderWerf, Pieter A. and John F. Mahon (1997)
**"Meta-Analysis of the Impact of Research Methods on Findings of
First-Mover Advantage"**
*Management Science*, Vol. 43, No. 11, pp. 1510-1519.
DOI: 10.1287/mnsc.43.11.1510
**Status: ABSTRACT ONLY.** Full text is paywalled at INFORMS/pubsonline;
no free full-text mirror was found. The abstract below is corroborated
identically across four independent citation sources (SSRN mirror listing,
Semantic Scholar, INFORMS's own abstract page, and a citing paper's
reference list), so the abstract wording itself is reliable — but do not
cite specific figures, tables, or methodology details from this paper
beyond what's in the abstract, because that has not been verified.

Abstract finding: tests of first-mover advantage that use **market share**
as the performance measure are "sharply and significantly more likely" to
find a first-mover advantage than tests using other measures such as
**profitability or survival**. Tests that sample from individually
selected industries, and tests that include no measures of the entrant's
competitive strength, were also significantly more likely to find an
advantage.

Note: Lieberman & Montgomery's own thought experiment (above) makes
essentially the same point with a concrete, verified illustration. Prefer
that for direct quoting/adapting; use VanderWerf & Mahon only as
supporting citation that this concern was later borne out empirically in
the literature, not as a source of specific figures.

## Browser wars (Netscape / Internet Explorer / Chrome)

Sources: TechRadar ("Internet Explorer cheated its way to the top, and I
won't miss it"), Visual Capitalist ("Internet Browser Market Share
1996-2019"), G2 Learn ("30 Years of Browser War"), Washington Post archive
("Microsoft's Courting of AOL Is Exhibit A," Oct 1998), InformationWeek
archive coverage of the Colburn testimony, and a Harvard cyber.law summary
of *U.S. v. Microsoft* trial evidence (citing Barksdale testimony and a
1998 internal Microsoft document entered as Government Exhibit 233).
**Status: read directly; cross-corroborated across multiple independent
outlets for the numbers, and the Harvard summary for the trial-evidence
specifics.**

Key facts:
- Netscape Navigator held ~80-90% browser market share by 1995-96.
- Netscape IPO'd ~16 months after founding, reaching a ~$3 billion
  valuation.
- March 1996: Microsoft and AOL reach a deal placing AOL's icon in
  Windows 95's "Online Services" folder on the desktop, in exchange for
  AOL making Internet Explorer its default/primary browser instead of
  Netscape Navigator.
- Trial testimony (Netscape CEO Jim Barksdale, per the Harvard summary):
  AOL's Steve Case and David Colburn told him AOL "would not have entered
  the agreement with Microsoft but for access to the Windows desktop."
- A 1998 internal Microsoft marketing document (DOJ Government Exhibit
  233): "It came with my computer, is the number one reason people switch
  to IE."
- Netscape's share fell to 42% by early 1999; IE climbed past 55% in the
  same period.
- By 2001, Netscape was in single digits; IE held roughly 90%.
- Firefox launched November 2004, grew meaningfully through the 2000s.
- Chrome launched September 2008, at a moment when IE's share had already
  slipped to roughly 74% (Firefox ~26%).
- Chrome overtook IE around 2012 (approx. 4 years after launch).
- By 2015, IE had fallen to third place, behind Firefox.
- As of recent tracking (2026), Chrome holds roughly 70-71% global market
  share.

**Correction note:** an earlier draft of this project used a quote
attributed to AOL's David Colburn calling Microsoft's offer "mob-like."
That quote could not be verified against primary or near-primary sources
and should NOT be reused. The verified replacements are the Barksdale
testimony line and the 1998 internal Microsoft document quote, both
above.
