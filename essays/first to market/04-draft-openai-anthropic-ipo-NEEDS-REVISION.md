For most of 2026, the largest initial public offering in history was
supposed to belong to OpenAI. By September, it had handed that title to
a company that didn't exist until four years after OpenAI shipped its
first product.

The reason isn't financial distress, and it isn't government pressure.
It's a scoreboard that flipped while almost no one outside the industry
was watching.

First, what actually happened.

OpenAI spent early 2026 preparing bankers for a listing that could have
valued the company near $1 trillion. On September 12, in an interview
with Fortune, CEO Sam Altman confirmed it would not happen in 2026 at
all. "Right now would be an ill-advised moment to go public," he said,
pointing to unfinished safety and alignment work rather than market
conditions. Anthropic, the company Altman's own former colleagues left
OpenAI to start in 2021, was not applying the same brakes. It is still
marketing a listing this fall, expected to price before the November
midterms, at a valuation some bankers put north of $2 trillion.

One company built the category. The other is going public first.

The three explanations that don't hold up.

The obvious guesses all run into a wall the moment you check them
against a fact already sitting in public view.

It isn't cash burn. Every frontier AI lab is spending at a rate that
would have alarmed investors in any other industry, Anthropic included.
If burning capital disqualified a company from a listing, neither of
these two would be filing anything this decade.

It isn't a first-mover's regulatory penalty. The theory has surface
appeal: be first, and you become the government's example. But the
companies that have actually drawn sustained antitrust attention in
tech, Microsoft in the 1990s, Google and Amazon and Meta today, were not
the pioneers in their categories. Search existed before Google. Online
retail existed before Amazon. Social networking existed before Facebook.
Regulators go after scale and dominance, not chronological order, and by
September 2026 dominance in the metric that funds a company long-term,
revenue, no longer clearly belonged to OpenAI.

It isn't a government order. No agency told OpenAI to wait. Altman was
explicit that the delay was the company's own call, tied to the mood
around AI risk rather than any legal action pending against it.

Each of these assumes something outside OpenAI made the decision for it.
Nothing did.

What actually flipped.

Here's the detail that makes this more than a scheduling story: Anthropic
didn't out-ship OpenAI. It out-earned it, and only recently.

Fifteen months earlier, Anthropic's annualized revenue was $1 billion
against OpenAI's tens of billions, a gap nobody expected to close. By
May 2026, Anthropic's own Series H announcement put its run rate at $47
billion, ahead of OpenAI's roughly $25 billion, a figure later reporting
put closer to $41 billion against Anthropic's $74 billion by midyear.
The split matters more than the totals. OpenAI still commands the far
larger audience, some 900 million weekly ChatGPT users, built on free
consumer traffic that costs more to serve than it earns. Anthropic built
almost the opposite business: enterprise contracts and API usage, the
segment where a customer generates several times more revenue per query
and doesn't disappear the day a better free option shows up. By mid-2026,
Anthropic held the larger share of enterprise AI spending and of the AI
coding market specifically, the two segments an income statement
actually rewards.

Being first bought OpenAI the audience. It didn't buy the revenue mix
that makes a public market comfortable underwriting a trillion-dollar
filing during a moment this anxious.

Every serious study of market pioneers says some version of the same
thing, going back decades: pioneers fail at roughly six times the rate
of the companies that entered early but not first, and the effect is
sharpest in categories built on genuinely new technology rather than
incremental improvement, exactly the category a foundation model is.
Foundation models are about as "really new" a product as the last
thirty years have produced. The literature would have predicted friction
for whoever got there first. It arrived on schedule.

The company that filed anyway.

What makes this worth more than an "I told you so" for pioneer theory is
that Anthropic isn't proceeding because it's unbothered by the thing
spooking OpenAI. Dario Amodei, Anthropic's own CEO, has been publicly
louder than almost anyone about the danger of moving too fast, going as
far as calling for the industry to slow down. He is making that argument
in public while his own company markets a stock offering.

That is not a contradiction so much as a position only the follower
could afford to take. Anthropic built its case for caution from second
place, where "we take this seriously" reads as differentiation. OpenAI
built its dominance from first place, where the same statement reads as
an admission about the thing it built.

OpenAI didn't lose anything. It moved a decision back a year.

The practical read for anyone watching this space isn't that OpenAI made
a mistake, or that Anthropic made the braver bet. It's that "first" and
"biggest" and "safest to bet on right now" turned out to be three
different companies, and the market spent eighteen months assuming
they'd stay the same one.
