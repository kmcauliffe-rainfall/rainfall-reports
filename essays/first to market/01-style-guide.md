# Style Guide: Rainfall Reports Narrative Articles

Distilled from Kyle's direct critique of two draft attempts. This is a
distillation, not a replacement for reading the actual reference article
(see 00-project-brief.md). When in doubt, match the reference article over
these rules.

## The core structural pattern (from the reference article)

1. **One throughline, not a mix.** Pick one mechanism, one system, one
   "board" the whole piece plays out on. The reference article is entirely
   about one thing: a stratified random sampling lottery. It does not
   digress into three unrelated case studies. A first attempt at this
   article used four unrelated cases (Fairchild/TI, Netscape, MySpace,
   OpenAI/Anthropic) and Kyle flagged this directly: "the other ones just
   seem like a mix." Cut to one spine. Everything else is at most a brief
   supporting beat or a closing callback, not a parallel narrative.

2. **It reads like a game, with rules, players, and a standout player.**
   The reference article works because it has:
   - A clear game board with real stakes (which metro areas are in vs. out)
   - Explicit rules (the stratified sampling mechanism, the 20-50% odds)
   - Specific numbers functioning as a scoreboard (720 hospitals, 107 in
     California, 37 Bay Area, 30 Inland Empire, 15 San Diego, 1 LA)
   - One standout "player" who breaks the pattern in a memorable way
     (Cedars-Sinai volunteering) — this is the emotional payoff, the
     "cherry on top"
   - A twist that rules out the easy wrong answer using a real control
     case (the SF/Bay Area comparison disproves "penalized for prior
     exposure")
   Every piece in this project should have this shape: rules, players,
   stakes, a standout, a twist.

3. **Open with "the reason isn't X, and it isn't Y. It's Z."** Establish
   the naive wrong answers before revealing the real one. Debunk them
   quickly and specifically, not with hand-waving.

4. **Short, declarative sentences functioning as section breaks.** The
   reference article uses lines like "Medicare drew the map at random." and
   "The hospital that signed up anyway." as mini-headers. Match this. Don't
   use literal bolded headers with generic labels ("Background," "Analysis").

5. **The closing line should be the thesis, stated plainly, not a vague
   flourish.** Kyle's exact feedback on the second draft: "the best line in
   your AI thing was the last line... just really focus in on that last
   line." Whatever the piece's real, specific, falsifiable claim is, that
   claim should be sitting in the final one or two sentences, plainly
   stated, not buried in the middle and replaced by something softer at
   the end.

## Sourcing and specificity rules

6. **Primary sources, not summaries of summaries.** If citing a paper,
   citing means having actually read the paper (or at minimum its full
   text, not just an abstract or a blog's paraphrase of it). Kyle's direct
   feedback: "you talk about the paper but it doesn't sound like you read
   it... you don't give any figures, don't give any specifics." When a
   full text genuinely can't be accessed (paywalled, no free mirror), say
   so explicitly rather than presenting a secondhand paraphrase as if it
   were verified.

7. **Numbers should be as specific as the reference article's.** "37 Bay
   Area hospitals" beats "many hospitals in the Bay Area." A 47% failure
   rate beats "pioneers often fail." When a real source gives a granular
   breakdown (e.g., 67% failure for durable goods vs. 28% for nondurable),
   use the granular version, not just the headline number.

8. **Flag what's uncertain instead of smoothing it over.** If a quote
   can't be verified, don't use it — find the real sourced version or drop
   it. If a statistic varies by source (e.g., different outlets reporting
   30% vs. 54% for the same market-share figure), say it varies and give a
   range, don't pick one number and present it as precise.

## Tone and language rules

9. **No "here's what you need to do" phrasing.** Kyle's direct note: "I
   would get rid of certain phrases that's like here's what you need to
   do." This is not a how-to guide or a listicle. It's a narrative
   explaining something true and specific.

10. **Avoid empty repetition-with-overstatement.** Repetition used to
    escalate toward a real point (see rule 3) is good. Repetition that just
    restates the same claim with bigger adjectives is not.

11. **Not agenda-pushing, not encyclopedia-boring.** Match the "Snapple cap
    fact" register: plain, confident, delivering one real insight per
    section, written for someone smart and busy who will notice if you're
    padding or spinning.

12. **Direct answers, no over-explanation**, per Kyle's general standing
    preference. Say the thing. Don't hedge every sentence.
